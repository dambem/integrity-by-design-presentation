01 · Autoregression

Listening to machines

An autoregressive model makes one modest claim: the recent past of a signal is the best guide to its next value. Take a sliding window of recent readings, learn a weighted combination of them, and you have a forecast.

Fig. 1a — the sliding window
yt−3 yt−2 yt−1 — w₁ w₂ w₃ → ŷt prediction = weighted sum of the window · the weights are what training learns

On its own a forecast is unremarkable. The trick is to flip it round: compare what the model expected with what actually arrived. The gap is called the residual. While the plant behaves normally the residual hovers near zero. When a failure starts to develop — a coupling wearing, a line beginning to occlude — the signal drifts away from its own history and the residual climbs, often days before anything trips.

Fig. 1b — live anomaly detector · WVP feed line (simulated)
RESIDUAL 0.00 · NOMINAL

SOLID — actual signal  ·  DASHED — model prediction ± confidence  ·  LOWER TRACK — residual vs alert threshold. Inject a fault and watch the residual cross the line before the signal looks visibly wrong. That gap is the maintenance window.

Why it mattered at WVP

The Waste Vitrification Plant turns highly active liquor into glass — one of the site's highest priorities. Every hour of unplanned downtime extends the life of the hazard. The success metric was never accuracy; it was lead time: enough warning to intervene before a blockage manifests.

Failures rhyme, but never at the same tempo

The same failure precursor can play out over hours or days. Dynamic time warping stretches and compresses two sequences to align their shapes, so a slow-motion replay of a known precursor still matches.

02 · Small language models

Reading the site

A language model is an autoregressive model wearing a different outfit. Same machinery: a window of recent context, a prediction of what comes next. The difference is that the “values” are now tokens — chunks of text — and the relationships between them are vastly richer than between two pressure readings, so the model needs far more capacity to learn them.

Sellafield's Performance Improvement process generates Condition Reports — free-text accounts of anything off-normal, raised by any of 11,000 people on site. Each one needs a trend code so patterns can surface. Coding by hand is slow; backlogs grow. A model that has learned the structure of language can read a report and predict its code the same way it would predict the next word.

Why small is the whole story

Nuclear data carries formal security classifications. No public APIs, no cloud — the model lives on a box in a locked room. That fixes the compute budget before a single experiment runs, and the engineering problem becomes: how much language understanding fits in the hardware you're allowed to have? A fine-tuned small model hit human-level coding at well under a second per report.

What the model actually learned

Not rules. Nobody wrote “vibration ⇒ equipment reliability.” The model absorbed thousands of historically coded reports and learned the statistical texture of each theme — including site dialect, abbreviations, and the difference between a hazard observed and a hazard caused.

Now scale the same predictor a thousandfold — and something new switches on.
03 · Large language models

Reasoning across jurisdictions

Grow the parameter count from millions to billions and the context window from one report to entire libraries, and next-token prediction stops looking like classification and starts looking like reading comprehension at scale: holding two long documents in mind at once and articulating how they relate.

A concrete use: comparing nuclear regulation across the Atlantic. The UK regime is goal-setting — it tells the licensee what must be achieved and leaves the how to them. The US regime is largely prescriptive — it specifies intervals, methods and thresholds. Cross-walking the two is months of expert reading. An LLM can hold both regimes in context and surface the correspondences for an expert to verify.

The same constraint, harder

Everything from level two still applies: classified-adjacent material, no public endpoints. The deployment question — where is the compute allowed to live? — now dominates the model question. Secure on-premises or self-managed infrastructure is the price of admission.

And a new failure mode

An anomaly detector fails quietly; an LLM fails fluently. Confident, plausible, wrong. In a regulated industry that means the model proposes and the human disposes — every cross-reference it surfaces is a lead for an expert, never a conclusion.

The lift

One idea, three altitudes

Lay the three side by side and the pattern is hard to unsee. Each level is the previous one with more context and more capacity — and at each step, what the model can be trusted with changes.
